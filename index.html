Introduction to our Dataset

We used the Chetty, Deming & Friedman’s college admissions study dataset. This dataset attempts to answer the questions of whether colleges "currently have many more students from high-income families than low-income families" and whether they could "increase the socioeconomic diversity of America’s leaders by changing their admissions policies?" Through the data they "show that attending an Ivy-Plus college instead of the average highly selective public flagship institution increases students’ chances of reaching the top 1% of the earnings distribution by 60%, nearly doubles their chances of attending an elite graduate school, and triples their chances of working at a prestigious firm". We felt that people need to be more aware of these systemic and economical issues which is what inspired us to create our visualizations from this data.

Design Decision Rational

We have chosen to create 4 visuals: a dot plot, map plot, bar plot, and pie plot.

We first wanted to show viewers how economics affect selectivity of college admissions. To do this, we chose a dot plot to easily allow viewers to compare admission rates and income status of admittees' families. Since this plot shows all of the data points the viewer is currently examining, we thought it would be a good idea to use it as a filtering tool as well. Additionally, we added encodings to allow the viewer to easily understand the information we wanted to convey. This plot contains color encodings, to distinguish between Public and Private colleges. Furthermore, the user can hover over data points to give an outline to the data point as well as opening up a popup to see the name of the college, the admission rate, and the breakdown of low vs. high income admittee families. Clicking on a data point instead of hovering keeps that popup open after moving the mouse off of the data point until you click again. Finally, this plot acts as a way to easily select groups of colleges to update the following charts with only data pertaining to those colleges through clicking and dragging inside the plot. We initially considered using color as a way to denote something about economic status but realized that position was a better indicator for it. Also, we thought about having onclick add/remove from the filtering but decided to just use a click and drag approach as we wanted to use the onclick to display some information about the selected college below the chart.

For our second graphic, we chose a US map because our data is very geographically relevant. The map is broken into states, with a color coding to distinguish the percentage of entrants to the state's population. We decided to do this because it allows a quick glance to inform viewers instead of having to read a chart of numbers or click on induvial states to read their information. However, we decided to also allow a viewer to click on a state for more details. This is so we don't clutter our graphics while still allowing viewers to learn more about each state's admissions if they want. Similarly, we show information on hover too. We also added a highlight of the state's borders on click to allow for easy distinguishing of which state was currently selected. We didn't actually consider any alternatives to these encodings or interactions because this was how we initially envisioned the graph as it felt the most intuitive.

For our third graphic, we wanted to show a breakdown of enrollment by race. Since there were only a few different races in our dataset, we thought it would be best to show them as a bar chart. Similar to our other graphics so far, hovering over a bar gives more information on it while clicking on it gives it an outline. This is to reduce cluttering the graph while also allowing the viewer to learn more if they want and is an intuitive thing seen in many digital graphs. We decided to put the races on the y axis and the percentage or enrollment on the x axis to allow for easy reading of the races and then the percentage the bar takes up. We had these axis initially flipped, but since you read left to right we realized it was better to have the axis as they are now.

Finally, for our fourth graph we wanted to show the breakdown of the share of students from each national parent-income quintile. We debated for a bit on which type of graph to use, but ultimately settled on a pie chart because it shows percentage comparisons out of 100% very well. We color coded each of the quintiles and added a key so it is very easy to see the individual quintile breakdown. There is also a hover and click feature just like in the other visualizations.

Development Process

We initially met to discuss which dataset we wanted to use and then discussed what we wanted to tell through our visualizations. After that, we worked together to come up with ideas behind our website and how it should look and then used AI to implement it exactly how we wanted. We spent around 5 hours developing our website. The aspect that took the most time was the design for the website and thinking through all the visual encodings and interactions we wanted to add.

AI Usage

We used Codex to implement our ideas. We told it exactly what we wanted from our graphs and such and it wrote all the code. We then made minor changes to small UI things like text size and graph colors.

We also used Claude (Anthropic's Claude Code) later on to fix issues with our graphs, such as the tooltip getting stuck open and the race bar chart colors and axis label.
